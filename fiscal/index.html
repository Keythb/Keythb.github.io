EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

IS THIS WHAT THE START OF A SOVEREIGN DEBT CRISIS LOOKS LIKE?
No — nothing here looks like the start of a sovereign debt crisis today.
But the system is running with less shock-absorbing capacity than usual: composite 50 (WATCH), 3 of 7 plumbing stress flags up. That is strain, not crisis — the difference is that every channel below is still functioning. Strain matters because it decides how well the system would take a shock, not because it causes one.
A sovereign funding crisis can only arrive through one of four doors — buyers refusing new auctions, existing holders selling the stock, the funding market seizing, or the interest arithmetic compounding. Where each stands today:
AUCTION DEMANDSOLID — A debt crisis starts here, as a buyers' strike: auctions tailing, dealers forced to absorb what investors refuse. Today buyers are showing up — foreign (indirect) bidders took 75.8 % of recent long-end auctions, bid-to-cover is 2.61 x, and dealers were left with only 5.0 %. Nothing in the auction tape resembles a buyers' strike.
FOREIGN HOLDERSSELLING — A crisis can also arrive from the stock rather than the flow: existing holders selling, which no auction statistic can see. NY Fed custody for foreign officials — where a central bank selling Treasuries to defend its currency shows up within days — moved -25 $B over four weeks; Japan, the largest foreign holder and the live intervention case, moved -75 $B over three months on the lagged TIC data (part of that is yields marking the stock down, not selling). Custody falling at this pace is what an official-sector selldown looks like while it is happening — this door is open, watch it weekly.
FUNDING PLUMBINGSTRESSED — The third route is a funding-market seizure — cash refusing to flow against good collateral, the 2019-repo shape. 3 of 7 stress flags are up (Curve noise, Discount window, SOFR scarcity), and the overnight buffer that used to absorb these shocks is effectively spent (ON RRP at 0 $B), so strain now transmits instead of being absorbed. Three or more flags is the zone that historically carried roughly twice the normal hazard of a market stress event over the following quarter — elevated odds of turbulence, not a prediction, and most such episodes resolve without one. SOFR scarcity is the one flag here with stand-alone predictive value for funding blowups, and it is up.

How to read it. Each row is one market-stress event in the 63 trading days after a given day. Base is the share of all days since 2014 that were followed by it; by day is the share of days with 3 or more flags up, with the multiple of base in parentheses; by episode counts each run of flagged days once, scored from its first day — the honest unit, because neighboring days share most of their window; 90% range is a 63-day block bootstrap on the by-day rate. Read the gap between the by-day and by-episode columns: the wider it is, the more a few long episodes carry the result. These are odds of turbulence, not of a sovereign debt crisis, which has no US case in the sample.
DEBT ARITHMETICCORROSIVE — The last route is not an event but arithmetic: debt carrying an average coupon of 3.52% reprices toward a 10-year at 5.00% — $10.6T of it within twelve months, and interest already absorbs 19.8 % of federal receipts. The one genuinely favorable structural reading: nominal growth still outruns the average coupon (r−g at -3.30 pp), which keeps this corrosive rather than explosive. It cannot cause a crisis this month; it makes every future shock harder to absorb, and it does not care what the rest of this page says.

The model’s medium-term index is 0.5 against a 0.40 high-risk cutoff (“marginally high” in the IMF’s words), driven by average borrowing needs of 46.2% of GDP a year and a 97.6% probability that debt does not stabilize. Only 3.4% of the IMF’s no-stress country-years read this high. In its calibration history, stress followed within five years about 20% of the time at the high-risk cutoff and 43% across the high zone on average — pooled over mostly emerging markets, in a sample with no stress episode for the US, UK, Japan, Germany or France. Staff overrode the model to low overall, citing “the strength of institutions, the depth of the investor pool, and the role of the US dollar in the international system.” In the 2024 Article IV the model read moderate.
How to read it. The model column is the mechanical signal of the IMF’s Sovereign Risk and Debt Sustainability Framework, estimated on past sovereign stress episodes (defaults, large IMF programs, spread spikes, loss of market access); the staff column is the IMF team’s final judgment after weighing what the model cannot see. Low, moderate and high are zones set so that about 10% of past crises fell in the low zone and about 10% of non-crises in the high zone. It is a slow, annual reading, refreshed with each US Article IV, and the only figure on this page calibrated on actual sovereign crises.
